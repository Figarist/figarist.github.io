The first online programming lesson can go two ways. In one, the child spends the hour coding and leaves excited. In the other, half the lesson disappears into a microphone that does not work, a program that will not install and screen sharing that is blocked.

The difference is almost always preparation. Before every new student starts, I talk with the parents on Telegram about age, time zone, computer and goals. This article collects what I check in those conversations, so you can get ready in advance. If you have not chosen a direction yet, start with the [parent guide to choosing a programming track](https://sivochka.com/blog/how-to-choose-programming-for-child/).

---

## What does a child need for any online coding lesson?

My lessons run on Zoom with screen sharing. Whatever the direction, these basics matter:

- **A computer or laptop** with Windows or macOS. For Scratch a tablet also works; for the other directions it does not.
- **A stable internet connection.** If Wi-Fi is weak in the child's room, try the lesson closer to the router once before the real lesson.
- **Zoom installed and updated**, with screen sharing allowed. On a Mac, Zoom needs permission to record the screen in *System Settings → Privacy & Security*. Without it, the child cannot show their screen.
- **A microphone.** The built-in one is usually fine. **Headphones** help a lot: they stop echo and make it easier for the child to hear me.
- **A camera is optional.** Many children are more relaxed without it, and the screen is what we work with anyway.
- **A mouse.** It is not strictly required, but for dragging Scratch blocks or working in Unity, a mouse is much more comfortable than a touchpad.
- **Permission to install programs.** For Python, Minecraft and Unity we need to install software, so the child's user account needs that right, or a parent nearby to approve it.
- **A quiet place at a desk.** Lying on a sofa with a laptop works for watching videos, not for writing code.

### Why install Unity before the first lesson?
The Unity Editor is a large download. If it installs during the lesson, we lose time that could go into the child's first game. I recommend installing Unity Hub and the Editor a day or two before and opening it once to check that it starts. If you are unsure whether the computer is powerful enough, the [Can I Run Unity? checker](https://sivochka.com/canirunUnity/) gives a quick answer in the browser.

### Do I need to buy anything?
For Scratch, Python and Unity, the software we use is free. Python through Minecraft is the exception: it needs a paid Minecraft Java Edition licence. If your child already plays the Java Edition on a computer, that licence is what we use.

## How does the first lesson go?

The first lesson is a full 60-minute lesson with practice, not a sales presentation. Here is the usual shape:

1. **A short check at the start.** We make sure sound and screen sharing work and say hello. This takes a few minutes if everything was prepared.
2. **The child shares their screen and works themselves.** I guide, ask questions and explain, but the child's hands are on the keyboard and mouse. That is how they remember things.
3. **We start practice straight away.** In Scratch, the child builds an interactive scene with a character they control. In Python through Minecraft, we begin with short coordinate commands that place blocks in the world. In Python, the child writes and runs their first small program. In Unity, we get to know the editor and make an object move, so the first playable mini-prototype comes in the early lessons.
4. **I watch how the child thinks.** What do they already know? What excites them? Where do they hesitate? This tells me much more than any test.
5. **We finish with a plan.** We agree on what to build next, so the child leaves with an idea for their own project.

### Should parents sit next to the child?
For younger children, it helps if a parent is nearby for the first few minutes to deal with any technical issue. After that, I suggest letting the child work on their own. Most children talk more freely and ask more questions when they are not being watched.

## What should parents expect after the first month?

Please do not expect a finished game after a month. I do not make promises like that, because every child learns at their own pace. Here is what a good first month looks like in my lessons:

- **The child can explain what they built.** Ask them to show you their project and tell you how it works.
- **New words appear naturally**: variable, loop, condition, sprite, script.
- **There is one project that keeps growing**, rather than many unrelated exercises.
- **The child asks more questions** and tries ideas on their own.
- **Lessons do not feel like a chore.** If your child dreads them, tell me: we can change the pace, the project or the direction.

Homework is optional and depends on what the child and the parents want. Between lessons, the student and the parents can message me on Telegram with questions.

## What are the most common first-lesson problems, and how do you avoid them?

- **Screen sharing is blocked** → check the Zoom permission (especially on a Mac) the day before.
- **A program updates or installs during the lesson** → install and launch everything once in advance.
- **Echo or poor sound** → use headphones.
- **The laptop slows down** → plug it in and close other programs and browser tabs.
- **A Minecraft or Unity version does not match** → tell me in advance which version is installed, and I will check it.

No. The initial conversation with parents on Telegram is free. The first full 60-minute lesson is paid at the standard rate (€20 / ~$22 / 1000 UAH).

**Does my child need a camera?**
No, a camera is optional. A microphone and screen sharing are what matter.

Tell me in advance. We will sort it out together at the start of the lesson.
